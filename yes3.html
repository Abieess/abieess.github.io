Elon Musk — First Principles

Physics thinking · Deleting the unnecessary · Rapid iteration

"Boil the exam down to its irreducible physics. Everything else is a convention someone else accepted."

The First-Principles Read

300 marks ÷ 180 minutes = 1.67 marks per minute. A one-mark question should cost you ~36 seconds. No negative marking means the expected value of every guess is positive — never leave a blank. Tamil is not "a section"; it is the battery pack. If the battery fails, the whole vehicle is stationary.

The 5-Step Algorithm (applied)

  • Make requirements less dumb: read Code 496 itself, not a YouTuber's summary.
  • Delete parts: cut 60% of your reference books. One textbook set, one guide, one PYQ compilation.
  • Simplify: one page of notes per unit, no more.
  • Accelerate cycle time: 25 minutes study → 5 minutes test → adjust.
  • Automate: flashcards and spaced repetition for facts that refuse to stick.

The Idiot Index

Divide (money + hours spent on a resource) by (marks it actually delivers). Free Samacheer PDFs have the lowest idiot index in this entire exam. Expensive coaching with no PYQs has the highest. Recalculate every book on your shelf.

What He'd Delete

  • Beautiful colour-coded notes
  • Highlighting as a study method
  • Watching study vlogs instead of studying
  • Twelve books on the same topic
  • Perfecting Unit I Science (5 questions) while Tamil bleeds

His 5 Daily Rules

  1. Question every "should" in your plan — who said you need that book?
  2. Every 25 minutes, close the book and test yourself.
  3. Measure marks-per-hour, not hours.
  4. If a study method isn't producing test results, kill it in one week.
  5. Failure is data. A bad mock is a sensor reading, not an identity.
Trap — Optimizing a part that shouldn't exist. Perfecting low-yield trivia while the 150-mark Tamil section stays untouched is the study equivalent of making a rocket engine prettier instead of lighter.

Mark Zuckerberg — Growth Hacking

Instrumentation · Feedback loops · Ship daily

"If you can't see the number, you can't improve the number. What gets measured gets hacked."

The Retention Dashboard

One page, six numbers, updated daily and reviewed Sunday:

  • Questions attempted today
  • Accuracy % (per subject)
  • Average seconds per question
  • 7-day retention % (re-test old topics)
  • Latest Tamil sectional score
  • Latest full-mock score

The Social Graph of Knowledge

Facts are nodes; connections are edges. Every new fact must link to three existing ones. Article 21 connects to DPSP, to Supreme Court judgments, to TN local governance. Isolated facts decay; connected facts compound.

A/B Testing Your Methods

Take one chapter. Study half by reading, half by active recall + questions. Re-test both after 7 days. Keep the winner, delete the loser. Stop guessing which method works — run the experiment.

"The Question Is the Algorithm"

PYQs are the ranking algorithm of this exam. They tell you exactly what the examiner rewards. Don't study the syllabus as a book — study it as a feed that the question-setter up-ranks.

His Operating Rhythm

  1. Ship daily: minimum 20 questions even on the worst day. No zero days.
  2. Fast feedback: never study a topic for more than 48 hours without testing it.
  3. Loop, don't line: mock → error log → targeted revision → mock.
  4. Public commitment: tell one person your weekly target. Accountability is a growth lever.
  5. Kill vanity metrics: hours studied is not a metric. Correct answers is.
Trap — Stealth mode. Studying for weeks with no mock feedback is like shipping code with no analytics. You'll feel productive and learn nothing about what's broken.

Jeff Bezos — Working Backwards

Start at the result · Small teams · The flywheel

"Write the press release from results day first. Then reverse-engineer every input that makes it true."

The Fake Press Release

"On 10 January 2027, I cleared TNPSC Group IV with 240/300." Now work backwards: Tamil 135/150 · GS 80/112.5 · Aptitude 25/37.5 → questions correct per unit → topics required → daily output required. Every study session must trace back to a line in that release.

The Two-Pizza Rule

Any task must be small enough for two people to finish over pizza. Not "finish Polity" — instead: "Articles 12–35 in 40 minutes, then 10 questions." Small, shippable, verifiable.

Type 1 vs Type 2 Decisions

  • Type 1 (irreversible): which books, which exam date, Tamil or no Tamil. Decide once, slowly, then stop.
  • Type 2 (reversible): study order, daily timings, note format. Decide fast and change freely.

Single-Threaded Leadership

One obsession per time block. When you study Polity, Polity is the only thing in the building. Multitasking is a meeting that never ends.

His Flywheel

  1. Better Tamil → confidence rises.
  2. Confidence → more willing to sit GS sessions.
  3. More GS → better mock scores.
  4. Better mocks → sharper Tamil revision targets.
  5. Loop repeats, each turn faster. Never break the wheel for a day.
Trap — Day-1 optimization. Spending two weeks perfecting the study plan is procrastination wearing a business suit. Ship a rough plan and iterate.

Feynman + Darwin — The Empirical Method

Hypotheses · Falsification · The lab notebook

"You don't know it until you can explain it without notes. And the first rule of the notebook is: write down the fact that ruins your theory."

The Feynman Audit

After every unit: close the book, take a blank page, write everything you know in plain language as if teaching a 10-year-old. Then open the book and diff your page against it. The gaps are your real syllabus.

Darwin's Golden Rule

Whenever a fact contradicts what you believed, record it immediately. Your Error Lab notebook is the most valuable book you own — every wrong answer is a confirmed falsification, which is far more informative than a right answer.

Controlled Experiments

Study Chapter A with notes, Chapter B without. Test both after 7 days. Whichever retains better becomes your permanent method. Don't assume — measure.

Replication Schedule

Recall the same material on Day 1, 2, 4, 7, 14. Science progresses by replication, not by one dramatic reading session.

His Scientific Protocol

  1. Spend 70% of revision time on wrong answers, not on things you already know.
  2. Teach one topic aloud every day — to a friend, a mirror, or a recorder.
  3. Never mark a topic "done" without a closed-book brain dump.
  4. Keep the Error Lab indexed by cause: didn't know · confused · misread · guessed.
  5. Treat confidence as a hypothesis, not evidence.
Trap — The illusion of fluency. Re-reading and highlighting create familiarity, which feels exactly like knowledge but is confirmation bias in disguise.

The Chess Grandmaster — Pattern, Tempo, Prophylaxis

Opening · Middlegame · Endgame · Blunder audit

"The exam is 200 tactical positions. You lose not to brilliant moves by the examiner, but to your own blunders on the clock."

Three Phases of the Game

  • Opening (Days 1–30): develop pieces — Tamil, Polity, TN History.
  • Middlegame (31–55): tactics — PYQs, elimination, calculation.
  • Endgame (56–80): precision — full mocks, time control, zero blunders.

Pattern Library

200 questions are 200 recurring patterns. Every PYQ you solve adds a pattern. By Day 60 you should recognise question shapes — the "which is NOT" shape, the "match the following" shape, the date-trap shape.

Calculation & Elimination

In every MCQ, eliminate two options fast, then compare the remaining two on one specific detail. Never re-derive from scratch under time pressure.

Prophylaxis — Prevent Your Own Blunders

  • Read the stem twice; highlight NOT / EXCEPT / ONLY.
  • Check the unit asked (Tamil grammar questions love single-letter traps).
  • Never change an answer without a concrete reason.

Tempo Rules

  1. Average budget: 54 seconds per question.
  2. Bank time on easy questions; spend it on the 3–4 genuinely hard ones.
  3. If a question exceeds 90 seconds, mark it and move — return later.
  4. Practise every mock with a visible clock.
Trap — Playing beautiful chess in your head. Perfect notes, deep understanding, zero timed practice. The clock is the opponent you're actually facing.

Sun Tzu — Terrain and Concentration of Force

Know the ground · Win before fighting · Logistics

"The victorious strategist wins before the battle. The defeated one fights first and hopes to win later."

The Terrain Map

  • The fortified gate: Tamil, 150 marks with a qualifying threshold.
  • The high ground: GS Units IV–VI — 55 of 75 questions.
  • The open flank: Aptitude, 25 cheap marks with daily practice.
  • The swamp: low-yield trivia and endless reference material.

Concentration of Force

Mass 60–70% of GS effort at Units IV–VI. Do not spread evenly across six units like a thin line of soldiers. A concentrated attack wins; an even line is broken everywhere at once.

Win Before Fighting

The exam is paperwork. The battle is preparation. By the time you sit in the hall, the outcome is largely already determined — by 80 days of logistics, not by adrenaline on the day.

The Five Factors

  • Tao (purpose): why you're doing this — write it down.
  • Heaven (timing): 80 days, fixed. Respect it.
  • Earth (terrain): the syllabus and weightage.
  • General (you): strengths and weaknesses, honestly assessed.
  • Method (discipline): daily routine, mock schedule, revision calendar.

Logistics Rules

  1. Sleep is a weapon. Under-slept soldiers lose winnable battles.
  2. Practise OMR shading — 200 bubbles on paper, not in theory.
  3. Protect your eyes: 20-20-20 rule during long reading sessions.
  4. Eat and hydrate before every mock as you would before the real exam.
  5. Never fight on all fronts at once.
Trap — Siege warfare. Spending three days on one difficult, low-yield topic because of ego. The strategist abandons a fortress that costs more than it yields.

Munger & Buffett — Inversion and Compounding

Margin of safety · Opportunity cost · Circle of competence

"Invert, always invert. Ask how you would guarantee failure — then simply avoid that list."

The Inversion List (print it)

  1. Skip Tamil because "I already speak it."
  2. Change books every two weeks.
  3. Never take a full mock.
  4. Study only what you already enjoy.
  5. Leave blanks because of fear.
  6. Read without solving questions.
  7. Ignore current affairs.
  8. Cram new topics in the final week.
  9. Study by hours, not by output.
  10. Study alone with no feedback.

Margin of Safety

The Tamil threshold is 40%. Aim for 80%. The extra 40% is a buffer that absorbs a bad paper, a bad night, a bad question set. Never aim for the minimum in a subject that can disqualify you.

Opportunity Cost Ledger

Every hour spent on Unit I Science (5 questions) is an hour not spent on Unit V Economy (20 questions). Write the ledger weekly. Which hour produced the most marks?

Compounding

30 questions a day × 80 days = 2,400 questions. Knowledge compounds like interest — slowly at first, then steeply. The mistake is quitting at Day 20 when the curve still looks flat.

His Checklist Discipline

  1. Circle of competence: mark every topic as known / shaky / unknown. Expand deliberately, don't drift.
  2. Use the same pre-mock checklist every single time.
  3. Most decisions are "no" — most study materials are also "no."
  4. Be patient with the plan, ruthless with the execution.
Trap — Activity bias and "diworsification." Doing something feels productive even when it's wrong; collecting fifteen sources feels like progress while none of them are finished.
